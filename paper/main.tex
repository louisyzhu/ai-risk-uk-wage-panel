\begin{abstract}
\noindent Occupations that the Office for National Statistics rated more automatable lost employment after 2016 and gained relative pay. We join the ONS automation probability to seven Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings releases for 366 UK occupations and find that log employee jobs fall by 0.483 log points per unit of probability, with a break at 2016 and a pre-period coefficient of the opposite sign. Mean pay rises by 0.122 along a path that begins before 2016 and that exposure to the National Living Wage accounts for. Estimating the pay margin alone reverses the sign of the labour-market story.
\end{abstract}

\noindent \emph{JEL codes:} J21, J23, J24, J31, J38, O33 \\
\emph{Keywords:} artificial intelligence, automation, automation exposure, labour demand, task displacement, occupational employment, wages, minimum wages, wage compression, event study, United Kingdom

\bigskip

Task-based models of automation make a prediction that empirical work on artificial intelligence (AI) has struggled to test. When capital can perform an occupation's tasks, the occupation loses labour demand. That loss appears as lower relative employment or lower relative pay, and the split between the two depends on how wages adjust \citep{acemoglu2019automation, acemoglu2022tasks}. Evidence on AI-exposed occupations remains thin on both margins. \citet{acemoglu2022vacancies} find that AI-exposed US establishments cut non-AI hiring after 2010 but detect no aggregate effect on employment or wages in exposed occupations. \citet{webb2020impact} finds declines in employment and pay for occupations exposed to software and industrial robots. In this paper we test the prediction for the United Kingdom. We follow employment and pay together. The two margins tell opposite stories until we hold the wage floor and the composition of occupations fixed.

To run the test, we join two public series on one occupational classification. The Office for National Statistics (ONS) assigns each four-digit occupation in the Standard Occupational Classification 2010 (SOC 2010) a probability of automation. The ONS built the probability with the method of \citet{frey2017future} and published it in 2019 on 2017 task data \citep{ons2019automation}. The Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) publishes mean and median gross hourly pay, the number of employee jobs, paid hours and a set of pay percentiles for the same occupations each April \citep{ons2024ashe}. The join yields 2,546 occupation-year cells for 366 occupations across the 2014 to 2020 releases. We stop at 2020 because those are the seven releases ASHE published on SOC 2010. In a working-paper version of this analysis we extended the panel to 2023 and read a positive post-2016 interaction between the probability and pay as evidence of augmentation. Here we restrict the panel to a single classification, add employment and hours, build the exposure to the National Living Wage (NLW) that a reviewer of that version asked for, and reach the opposite conclusion.

Our main finding is that employment fell in the occupations rated more automatable. On the 309 occupations with a published jobs count in every release, we regress log employee jobs on the probability interacted with a post-2016 indicator under occupation and release fixed effects. The coefficient is $-0.483$ with a standard error of 0.057 clustered by occupation. The standard deviation of the probability is 0.145. An occupation one standard deviation more automatable thus had employment about 7.0 log points lower after 2016 relative to before, or 6.8 per cent. The event study dates the fall. Relative to 2015, the 2014 coefficient is $+0.095$ (0.044). In other words, the more automatable occupations were gaining employment before the treatment date. The coefficients then run $-0.243$, $-0.245$, $-0.324$, $-0.494$ and $-0.869$ from 2016 to 2020. Total employment in the balanced panel grew from 24.7 to 26.5 million jobs over 2014 to 2019 while the share in above-median-risk occupations fell from 54.1 to 50.8 per cent. The pattern is reallocation across occupations within a growing total. When we condition on the occupation's exposure to the NLW, the estimate is $-0.536$ (0.072).

By contrast, pay in the same occupations rose, and the wage floor explains the rise. The same interaction for log mean gross hourly pay is 0.122 (0.019), or 1.8 per cent per standard deviation of the probability. The event study shows that the pay gradient in 2014 was 0.043 log points lower than in 2015 (0.017). That increment matches the 0.051 (0.014) gain from 2015 to 2016, and the gradient then rose by between 0.023 and 0.051 log points in each release to 2019. When we let the gradient trend linearly and add a 2016 break, the trend is 0.034 per year (0.007) and the break is 0.015 (0.020) over 2014 to 2019. We then measure each occupation's exposure to the floor as the share of its jobs paid below \pounds 7.20 in the 2015 release, the NLW rate of April 2016. Interacting that share with the post-2016 indicator lowers the pay coefficient from 0.132 (0.018) to 0.033 (0.021). Allowing the share its own path in every release leaves the pay event-study coefficients for 2016 to 2020 at $-0.002$, 0.003, 0.023, 0.051 and 0.005, none distinguishable from zero.

Taken together, the two margins describe an occupational labour market in which the more automatable occupations shed jobs after 2016 while a rising floor and the exit of the lowest-paid jobs lifted the pay of those who remained. In Appendix~\ref{app:theory} we set out a framework in which a demand shift against automatable tasks, a binding wage floor and a within-occupation pay distribution produce exactly this pattern. There we derive the share of jobs below the floor as the first-order effect of a floor increase on occupational mean pay. We also record one prediction that the data do not support. We caution against reading the employment result as a causal estimate of automation. We have no measure of technology adoption, and the probability is a 2017 assessment of task automatability that predates large language models. We can establish three things. The probability predicts relative employment loss after 2016 with no pre-trend of the same sign. It predicts faster relative pay growth only through the level of pay. And a single account reconciles both facts.

Accordingly, we make three contributions. First, we document the joint evolution of employment and pay across UK occupations ranked by a published automation probability over 2014 to 2020. Second, we show that an exposure-score difference-in-differences with a single post period recovers a pre-existing compression driven by the wage floor when estimated on pay alone. That design reverses the sign of the labour-market story. Third, we document in Section~\ref{sec:soc} and Appendix~\ref{app:soc} that joining releases published on SOC 2020 to SOC 2010 risk scores by numeric code attaches the score to a different occupation for 125 of 261 matched codes. This mismatch manufactured the largest coefficients in the earlier version. The second and third contributions apply to any study that joins an occupation-level exposure score to a national pay series.

Specifically, we pose three questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether the cross-occupation gradient between the automation probability and log pay changed after 2016, and whether the change is a break at 2016 or the continuation of a trend that predates it. \textbf{RQ2} asks how much of the pay movement exposure to the National Living Wage accounts for. \textbf{RQ3} asks whether employment and hours in more automatable occupations changed after 2016, and with what timing. Figure~\ref{fig:concept} states the three accounts of the pay gradient that RQ1 has to distinguish. In Section~\ref{sec:lit} we place the questions in the task-based, exposure-score and minimum-wage literatures. In Section~\ref{sec:data} we describe the panel and the exposure measure. In Section~\ref{sec:strategy} we set out the specifications and state what each identifies. In Section~\ref{sec:results} we answer the three questions. In Section~\ref{sec:sens} we report alternative specifications, in Section~\ref{sec:soc} we report the classification join, and in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} we state the limitations in decreasing order of consequence.

\section{Automation Exposure, Wage Floors and the UK Setting}\label{sec:lit}

Our question sits at the intersection of three literatures. The first is the task-based tradition. \citet{acemoglu2019automation} show that automation reduces the labour share in value added when capital acquires tasks that workers previously performed, and that it can reduce labour demand unless new tasks appear. \citet{acemoglu2022tasks} show that the reduction in demand appears as lower relative pay in the occupations that lose tasks. \citet{acemoglu2020robots} estimate that one additional industrial robot per thousand workers lowered wages in exposed US commuting zones by 0.42 per cent and lowered the employment-to-population ratio by 0.2 percentage points over 1990 to 2007. \citet{graetz2018robots} find productivity gains from robots across 17 countries and some reduction in the employment share of low-skilled workers. \citet{autor2003skill} supply the mechanism. Computers substitute for codifiable routine tasks and complement non-routine analytic ones. This tradition predicts that exposure lowers both employment and pay in the affected occupations. It does not ask how the two margins separate when the exposure measure is a published probability and the pay series sits under a binding statutory floor. We ask that here.

The second literature builds measures of exposure to AI specifically. We borrow its measurement logic and take no position on its contested pay findings. \citet{felten2021occupational} construct and validate the AI Occupational Exposure index. The index links advances in AI to the abilities an occupation draws on. \citet{webb2020impact} constructs exposure from the overlap between patent text and occupational task descriptions and finds that occupations highly exposed to earlier automation technologies saw declines in employment and pay. \citet{acemoglu2022vacancies} use the near-universe of US online vacancies to show that AI-exposed establishments reduced non-AI hiring after 2010 with aggregate effects too small to detect. \citet{eloundou2024gpts} rate 1.8 per cent of US jobs as having more than half their tasks affected by large language models with simple interfaces and 46 per cent once they allow for complementary software. That exposure profile rises with pay. In a field experiment, \citet{brynjolfsson2025generative} find that a generative-AI assistant raised the productivity of customer-support agents by 15 per cent with gains concentrated among less experienced workers. None of these studies uses a UK exposure measure or a UK pay panel. None follows employment and pay in the same occupations across a period in which a statutory floor rose.

Within this second literature, the ONS probability carries two limits that we must confront. The probability descends from the method of \citet{frey2017future}, who assigned 702 US occupations a probability of computerisation from expert classification of task bottlenecks. \citet{arntz2017revisiting} showed that moving the classification from occupations to individual jobs reduces the share of US jobs at high risk from 38 to 9 per cent, because workers within an occupation specialise in tasks that differ in how automatable they are. The ONS measure also predates large language models. It captures the automatability of 2017 tasks by the capabilities anticipated then. Both limits attenuate any technology signal in the probability. Neither removes its correlation with the level of pay, and that correlation is what we have to confront.

The third literature studies the UK wage floor. The NLW took effect in April 2016 at \pounds 7.20 for workers aged 25 and over, with a target of 60 per cent of median pay by 2020. It raised the floor by 7.5 per cent at introduction and by between 4.2 and 6.2 per cent in each subsequent April to 2020. \citet{giupponi2024minimum} exploit geographic variation in exposure to the national floor. They find a rise in pay at the bottom of the distribution with spillovers up to roughly the twentieth percentile and no statistically detectable fall in employment over 2016 to 2019. Pay at the bottom of the distribution belongs disproportionately to occupations the ONS rates as automatable. Any occupation-level pay series will consequently register the floor as faster growth in high-risk occupations. \citet{autor2023compression} document the same compression in the United States after 2020 with no change in the federal floor. They attribute it to tightness in the low-wage labour market and rising job-to-job separations. The minimum-wage literature does not ask what its findings imply for exposure-score studies of automation. Nor does it ask whether the occupations most exposed to the floor were also the occupations losing employment for other reasons. We ask both questions.

Finally, one econometric point joins the three literatures. \citet{roth2023trending} recommend that researchers accompany any static difference-in-differences estimate with event-study estimates and pre-trend evidence. The reader then sees when the outcome moved and whether it was already moving. \citet{callaway2024continuous} show that with a continuous treatment the two-way fixed-effects interaction identifies an average causal response only under a strong parallel-trends assumption. That assumption rules out selection into dose on the basis of the path an occupation would follow. The ONS probability is a continuous dose. It correlates with the level of pay, and the level of pay determines exposure to the floor. For the pay outcome the strong assumption thus fails by construction unless we hold the floor fixed. We respond in three ways. We report every static interaction as a description. We test its timing. And we condition on a predetermined measure of floor exposure.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

We build the panel from the ONS automation probability and five series in ASHE Table 14, all on the SOC 2010 four-digit classification and all for the 2014 to 2020 releases. ASHE samples 1 per cent of employee jobs from HM Revenue and Customs PAYE records with an April reference date. Table 14.5a reports the number of employee jobs in thousands, the median and mean of gross hourly pay, and the 10th, 20th, 25th, 30th, 40th, 60th, 70th, 75th, 80th and 90th percentiles for each occupation. Table 14.6a reports the same quantities for hourly pay excluding overtime, and Table 14.9a reports total paid hours per week. In Appendix~\ref{app:data} we list the workbook we read for every release and the number of cells each publishes. The ONS suppresses some cells for quality or disclosure. We treat those cells as missing and impute none.

Our primary pay outcome is $\ln w_{it}$, the log of nominal mean gross hourly pay in occupation $i$ and release $t$. We keep pay nominal. Every specification includes release fixed effects, and a common deflator enters log pay additively by release. Nominal and real specifications hence return identical interaction and event-study coefficients, as we show in Proposition~\ref{prop:deflator}. We use the median and the overtime-exclusive mean as alternative pay outcomes. Our employment outcome is $\ln N_{it}$, the log of employee jobs, and our hours outcome is the log of mean paid hours. The ONS suppresses small job counts. The jobs series covers fewer cells than pay as a result. Our employment analysis rests on 346 occupations with any published count and on 309 with a count in every release.

Our exposure measure is the ONS probability of automation, $A_i$, defined for 369 SOC 2010 unit groups \citep{ons2019automation}. The ONS applied the \citet{frey2017future} method to 2017 Annual Population Survey task data. It reported that 7.4 per cent of jobs in England were at high risk, defined as a probability above 0.7. Waiters and waitresses, shelf fillers and elementary sales occupations sit at the top of the distribution. Medical practitioners, higher-education teaching professionals and senior professionals of educational establishments sit at the bottom. Across the 366 occupations that match at least one pay cell, the probability ranges from 0.181 to 0.728 with a mean of 0.446 and a standard deviation of 0.145. It does not vary over time. Occupation fixed effects absorb it in levels, and all identifying variation comes from its interaction with time.

In addition, we need a measure of each occupation's exposure to the wage floor. We build it from the 2015 release so that it precedes the floor it measures. For each occupation we interpolate the share of employee jobs paid below \pounds 7.20, the NLW rate that took effect in April 2016, from the published percentiles of the 2015 release. We assume that pay follows a piecewise-linear distribution between adjacent published percentiles. In Appendix~\ref{app:data} we state the formula as equation (B1) and work two occupations through it by hand. We can compute the share for 347 occupations. It has a mean of 0.086 and a standard deviation of 0.145. It equals zero for the lowest-paid tenth of the distribution of occupations and 0.286 at the ninetieth percentile, and it correlates 0.60 with the automation probability. As a second measure we use the Kaitz index, namely \pounds 7.20 divided by the occupation's 2015 median hourly pay. The Kaitz index correlates 0.82 with the probability. We fix both measures at their 2015 values.

Table~\ref{tab:sample} describes the resulting panel release by release. Each release publishes a mean for between 362 and 365 of the 366 occupations, and 358 occupations have a mean in all seven releases. The cross-sectional slope of log pay on the probability is $-2.11$ (0.08) in 2014. An occupation one standard deviation higher in the probability paid 31 log points less in that year. The slope rises monotonically to $-1.92$ (0.08) in 2020. The final column reports the share of employee jobs in above-median-risk occupations. It falls from 54.1 per cent in 2015 to 50.8 in 2019 and 48.0 in 2020. Both series move before we estimate anything, and both are the objects our regressions model. Table~\ref{tab:desc} gives descriptive statistics for every variable.

\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{sec:strategy}

We estimate four specifications. We choose them to answer the three questions in order. Our static specification interacts the probability with a post-2016 indicator in a two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) regression,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:twfe}
y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta \left( A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2016] \right) + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $y_{it}$ is log pay, log jobs or log hours, $\alpha_i$ is an occupation fixed effect, $\lambda_t$ a release fixed effect, and $\varepsilon_{it}$ an occupation-year disturbance. The earlier version included a post-2016 main effect alongside the release effects. The two are collinear. The estimation software resolved the collinearity by dropping a release indicator while retaining the main effect. The reported release coefficients then had no interpretable base. We omit the main effect in equation~\eqref{eq:twfe} and define 2015 as the base release. This choice leaves $\beta$ unchanged. We weight each occupation-year equally in the main estimates. Under that weighting $\beta$ describes the average occupation, and we report employment-weighted estimates that describe the average worker alongside. We cluster standard errors by occupation. Clustering allows arbitrary serial correlation within an occupation's seven observations across 366 clusters.

\input{tables/desc}

We read $\beta$ as a description and not as a causal effect, for a reason specific to this setting. The coefficient measures the change from the pre-2016 releases to the post-2016 releases in the cross-occupation slope of the outcome on the probability. In Proposition~\ref{prop:static} we show that with equal weights and a balanced panel it equals the slope of a regression of each occupation's post-minus-pre mean change on its probability, and that it equals the average of the post-period event-study coefficients minus the average of the pre-period ones. \citet{callaway2024continuous} show that with a continuous treatment such a coefficient identifies an average causal response only under a strong parallel-trends assumption. That assumption requires the path an occupation would follow at any given dose to be independent of the dose it holds. The probability correlates at $-0.80$ with the 2014 level of pay, and the level of pay governs exposure to the NLW. The assumption fails for pay unless we hold the floor fixed. A rising floor can also reduce employment where it binds, and for that reason we apply the floor-exposure control to both margins.

Next, we replace the single indicator with a full set of release interactions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:es}
y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \sum_{s \neq 2015} \beta_s \left( A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t = s] \right) + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
so that $\beta_s$ is the change in the gradient between 2015 and release $s$. Only the 2014 release precedes the treatment date. The single pre-period coefficient $\beta_{2014}$ thus cannot support a formal test of parallel trends, as \citet{roth2023trending} note for short pre-periods. It can show whether the gradient was already moving before 2016 in the direction and at the rate it moved after. That is the comparison each margin needs. We also test the same point in one parameter. We let the gradient trend linearly and add a break,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trend}
y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \gamma \left( A_i \times (t - 2015) \right) + \delta \left( A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2016] \right) + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the annual movement in the gradient and $\delta$ any level shift at 2016 beyond the trend. Under a break, $\delta$ differs from zero and $\gamma$ is near zero. Under a steady movement, $\gamma$ differs from zero and $\delta$ is near zero. In Proposition~\ref{prop:restriction} we state equation~\eqref{eq:trend} as a restriction on the event-study coefficients and explain why $\delta$ is the less precisely estimated of the two.

Third, we condition on the floor. Let $F_i$ denote the occupation's 2015 share of jobs below \pounds 7.20, demeaned across occupations, and let $P_t = \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2016]$. We estimate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nlw}
y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta \left( A_i \times P_t \right) + \theta \left( F_i \times P_t \right) + \kappa \left( A_i \times F_i \times P_t \right) + \varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
Here $\theta$ captures post-2016 movement that varies with exposure to the floor, $\beta$ is the risk gradient's movement at mean exposure, and $\kappa$ tests whether the two exposures interact. We choose the share of jobs below the floor as the exposure measure for a structural reason. In Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} we show that this share is the first-order effect of a floor increase on an occupation's mean pay when the pay index and composition stay fixed. The coefficient $\theta$ thus has a reading in the model as well as in the regression. In an event-study version we interact $F_i$ with every release indicator and leave the floor path unrestricted. As a check we substitute the Kaitz index for $F_i$.

For inference, we cluster standard errors by occupation throughout. Because the treatment is a fixed occupation attribute, we also run a permutation test. We reassign the 366 probabilities at random across occupations 2,000 times and re-estimate equation~\eqref{eq:twfe} each time. The resulting distribution of $\hat{\beta}$ is the null distribution under the hypothesis that the probability carries no information about the outcome's dynamics.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Pay Gradient after 2016, and Whether It Broke or Trended}

We find that the pay gradient became less negative after 2016 on every definition of pay. Table~\ref{tab:pay} reports equation~\eqref{eq:twfe}. On the 2014 to 2020 panel the interaction is 0.122 (0.019) with a 95 per cent confidence interval of $[0.085, 0.158]$. We multiply by the occupation-level standard deviation of the probability, 0.145, and obtain 0.018 log points. An occupation one standard deviation more automatable thus saw mean pay about 1.8 per cent higher after 2016 relative to before, conditional on occupation and release effects. The estimate is 0.124 (0.018) on the 358 occupations present in every release and 0.116 (0.018) on the 2014 to 2019 releases. It is 0.139 (0.019) with the median in place of the mean and 0.124 (0.019) with hourly pay excluding overtime. Weighting by 2015 employee jobs raises it to 0.187 (0.025) against 0.134 (0.019) unweighted on the same 333 occupations. The compression is stronger among large occupations.

However, the movement did not begin in 2016. Table~\ref{tab:espay} and Figure~\ref{fig:espay} replace the single indicator with release-by-release coefficients. In column 1 the coefficient for 2014 is $-0.043$ (0.017). The gradient was 0.043 log points steeper in 2014 than in 2015. The coefficients for 2016 to 2020 are 0.051 (0.014), 0.073 (0.020), 0.101 (0.023), 0.151 (0.024) and 0.125 (0.030). The year-on-year increments are 0.043 from 2014 to 2015 and 0.051 from 2015 to 2016. They then run 0.023, 0.027 and 0.051, and $-0.027$ into 2020. We cannot distinguish the pre-treatment increment from the treatment-year increment in size. Nothing in the sequence marks 2016 as the release in which the gradient began to move. The employment-weighted column 2 has the same shape at larger amplitude, with $-0.063$ in 2014 and 0.233 in 2020.

In one parameter, Table~\ref{tab:trend} states the same finding. On the 2014 to 2019 releases the gradient trends at 0.037 (0.006) per year when we allow no break. When we add a 2016 break, the trend is 0.034 (0.007) and the break is 0.015 (0.020) with a confidence interval of $[-0.025, 0.054]$. \subsection{The Wage Floor}

We find that exposure to the wage floor accounts for the movement in the pay gradient. Table~\ref{tab:nlw} reports equation~\eqref{eq:nlw} on the 347 occupations for which we can compute the exposure share. Column 1 restates the pay interaction on this sample at 0.132 (0.018). Column 2 adds the interaction between the 2015 share of jobs below \pounds 7.20 and the post-2016 indicator. The risk coefficient falls to 0.033 (0.021) with an interval of $[-0.008, 0.074]$, and the floor coefficient is 0.164 (0.017). An occupation with a tenth more of its jobs below the incoming floor grew 1.6 log points faster after 2016. Once we allow for that, the probability adds nothing that the data can distinguish from zero. Column 3 adds the triple interaction. It is $-0.108$ (0.197) and uninformative. Columns 4 and 5 replace the share with the Kaitz index. The risk coefficient becomes $-0.047$ (0.031) and $-0.025$ (0.033), and the Kaitz interaction is 0.154 (0.026). The two exposure measures agree that the post-2016 pay movement belongs to the floor. In Appendix~\ref{app:theory} we work out what magnitude the floor coefficient should take under the framework. The estimate lies above the implied lower bound.

\input{tables/nlw}

Because the linear form in equation~\eqref{eq:nlw} could mask timing, we also let the floor path vary by release. In column 3 of Table~\ref{tab:espay} we interact the share with every release indicator. The risk coefficients for 2016 to 2020 become $-0.002$ (0.018), 0.003 (0.025), 0.023 (0.028), 0.051 (0.028) and 0.005 (0.033), against 0.051, 0.073, 0.101, 0.151 and 0.125 without the control. The 2014 coefficient moves from $-0.043$ to $-0.034$ (0.020). The pre-period difference is thus partly the floor as well. That reading is consistent with the National Minimum Wage upratings of October 2014 and October 2015 that preceded the NLW. In Appendix~\ref{app:extra} we report the earlier version's proxy, in which the occupation's 2014 log pay replaces the exposure share. It reaches the same reading with less precision. This answers RQ2 in full. Exposure to the wage floor accounts for the post-2016 movement in the pay gradient, and the automation probability carries no association with post-2016 pay growth beyond the one it shares with low pay.

\subsection{Employment and Hours}

Employment moved in the opposite direction to pay, and by more. Table~\ref{tab:jobs} reports equation~\eqref{eq:twfe} for log employee jobs and log hours. On the 346 occupations with any published jobs count the interaction is $-0.501$ (0.057). On the 309 occupations with a count in every release it is $-0.483$ (0.057) with an interval of $[-0.594, -0.371]$. An occupation one standard deviation more automatable had employment about 7.0 log points lower after 2016 relative to before, or 6.8 per cent, conditional on occupation and release effects. The estimate is $-0.374$ (0.055) on the 2014 to 2019 releases, which exclude the April 2020 reference date. It is $-0.359$ (0.054) when we weight by 2015 jobs. Column 5 adds the floor-exposure interaction. The risk coefficient is $-0.536$ (0.072) and the floor coefficient is 0.099 (0.059). Exposure to the floor does not account for the employment loss. If anything, the point estimate suggests that occupations more exposed to the floor lost slightly less. Hours in column 6 move little, at $-0.027$ (0.013).

Moreover, the raw counts tell the same story without a regression. Among the 309 balanced occupations, total employee jobs rose from 24.7 million in 2014 to 26.5 million in 2019. The share in above-median-risk occupations fell from 54.1 per cent in 2015 to 50.8 in 2019 and 48.0 in 2020. Employment in the lowest-risk tercile grew by 10.2 log points between 2015 and 2019 and in the middle tercile by 1.6. In the highest-risk tercile it fell by 3.2. Across the 324 occupations with both series in 2015 and 2019, the change in log employment correlates $-0.37$ with the probability and the change in log mean pay correlates $+0.36$. The two changes correlate $-0.06$ with each other. This answers RQ3 in full for employment and negatively for hours. Employment in more automatable occupations fell relative to others from 2016 onward after a pre-period in which it had been rising, and exposure to the wage floor does not explain the fall. Hours moved little until 2020.

Consequently, one account fits all three answers. We set it out in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}. A shift in labour demand against automatable tasks lowers employment in the occupations that hold them. It also lowers their wage index where the wage is free to fall. A statutory floor that binds for a share $F_i$ of the occupation's jobs prevents the fall for those jobs. It raises the observed mean by $F_i$ per unit increase in the floor, as we show in Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}. The exit of jobs paid below the occupation's mean raises the mean of those who remain, as we show in Proposition~\ref{prop:composition}. An exposure-score regression on mean pay alone consequently records a positive interaction from the floor and from composition. It reads a labour market in which the exposed occupations were losing employment as one in which they were gaining. On the employment margin, by contrast, the probability predicts what the task-based tradition expects.

\section{Alternative Specifications}\label{sec:sens}

We find that the pay result does not depend on how we define the treatment or the time controls. Table~\ref{tab:rob} collects the alternatives. Replacing the continuous probability with an above-median indicator gives 0.032 (0.006). Terciles give 0.010 (0.007) for the middle third and 0.036 (0.007) for the top third relative to the bottom. The movement is monotone in the dose. Replacing release fixed effects with major-group-by-release effects gives 0.186 (0.066). These effects allow each of the nine one-digit SOC groups its own pay path. Sub-major-group-by-release effects across 25 two-digit groups give 0.227 (0.084). Within a skill group the more automatable occupations are also the lower paid. These estimates are the within-group counterpart of the same floor-driven compression.

\section{The Classification Join}\label{sec:soc}

ASHE moved from SOC 2010 to SOC 2020 with the 2021 release. The ONS dataset page for Table 14 on the SOC 2010 basis continues to host the later releases. The workbook titles are the only warning. The workbooks inside the 2014 to 2020 archives carry SOC 2010 titles and those inside the 2021 to 2023 archives carry SOC 2020 titles. The 2021 release publishes a mean for 406 of 412 SOC 2020 unit groups. In the earlier version we joined the 2021 to 2023 releases to the SOC 2010 risk scores by numeric code. That join kept the 261 codes that exist in both classifications. Of those 261 codes, 136 carry the same occupation title under both classifications and 125 carry a different one. Roughly half the post-2020 rows thus attached a 2017 risk score to a different occupation. Table~\ref{tab:soc} lists examples. Code 1161 denotes managers in transport and distribution under SOC 2010 and officers in the armed forces under SOC 2020.

As a result, the join manufactured the largest estimates in the earlier version. In Table~\ref{tab:contam} and Figure~\ref{fig:contam} we reproduce its design. With the mismatched releases included, the static pay interaction is 0.224 (0.030) against 0.122 (0.019) on the single-classification panel. The event-study coefficients for 2021, 2022 and 2023 are 0.425, 0.495 and 0.521, against a maximum of 0.151 on the clean panel. We had read the post-2020 rise as the compression accelerating. It was the risk score attached to occupations it does not describe. Extending the panel past 2020 requires a crosswalk that allocates each SOC 2020 unit group to its SOC 2010 predecessors with employment weights. We do not attempt that here. The warning transfers. Any occupation-level exposure score is keyed to one classification. Any national series that changes classification mid-sample will match a large share of codes by number and a smaller share by meaning.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

We set out to test whether UK occupations that the ONS rated more automatable lost employment or pay after 2016. They lost employment. Log employee jobs in an occupation one standard deviation more automatable fell by about 7 log points relative to others after 2016. The fall shows a break at the treatment date and a pre-period movement of the opposite sign, and it does not change once we hold exposure to the wage floor fixed. Mean pay in the same occupations rose relative to others by about 1.8 per cent per standard deviation. That rise began between 2014 and 2015, moved linearly to 2019, and the share of the occupation's jobs paid below the incoming floor accounts for it. Our framework in Appendix~\ref{app:theory} reconciles the two margins. Demand shifted against automatable tasks. The floor held pay up where it bound and raised observed means in proportion to exposure. The exit of low-paid jobs raised the mean of those who remained. We had read the pay interaction as augmentation. We withdraw that reading. The evidence is consistent with displacement on the quantity margin. It is silent on augmentation because we have no measure of what the remaining workers produce.

Nevertheless, six limitations qualify these conclusions. We state them in decreasing order of consequence. First, we have no measure of technology adoption and no date at which adoption began. We cannot estimate a causal effect of AI on either margin. We can establish that the probability predicts relative employment loss with a break at 2016 and no pre-trend of the same sign, and that it predicts relative pay growth only through the level of pay. Second, our employment outcome is the published count of employee jobs from a 1 per cent sample with small counts suppressed. The balanced employment panel covers 309 of 366 occupations, and the excluded occupations are small. The unweighted, weighted and unbalanced estimates agree in sign and magnitude. That agreement limits the concern without removing it. Third, the 2020 release has an April reference date that falls in the first pandemic lockdown, when large numbers of employees were furloughed and hours fell sharply. The 2020 coefficients for employment and hours carry a shock unrelated to automation. We rest our conclusions on timing on the 2014 to 2019 estimates. Fourth, we interpolate the floor-exposure share from published percentiles and assume a piecewise-linear pay distribution within an occupation. The Kaitz index needs no interpolation and gives the same reading. Fifth, we weight each occupation equally in the main estimates. Those estimates describe the average occupation. Our employment-weighted estimates describe the average worker, and they are larger for pay and smaller for employment. Sixth, the probability is a 2017 measure that predates large language models. \citet{arntz2017revisiting} show that occupation-level probabilities overstate task-level risk. The measure is a noisy proxy for exposure to the technology now diffusing. None of these limitations touches the two findings that carry the paper. The pay gradient was moving before 2016 at the rate it moved after, and that rests on the two pre-treatment releases alone. Employment moved the other way.

Looking ahead, three extensions would take the analysis further. A crosswalk from SOC 2020 to SOC 2010 with employment weights would carry the panel to 2023 and beyond on a consistent basis. The ASHE microdata, available through the Secure Research Service, would replace occupation means with individual pay histories. That would separate incumbent pay growth from composition directly. It is the decomposition of Proposition~\ref{prop:composition} taken to the data. Our design also transfers to newer exposure measures. The AI Occupational Exposure index of \citet{felten2021occupational} and the language-model exposure of \citet{eloundou2024gpts} both rise with pay. Conditioning on the floor is the first check any UK application of them should run. The employment margin is where the check should look first.

In sum, the result that survives is a modest one. We state it at its strength. Over 2014 to 2020, UK occupations rated most automatable in 2017 lost employment relative to the rest from 2016 onward. They gained relative pay along a path that the wage floor explains. A single post-period regression on pay would have reported the opposite of what the employment series shows. Read together with the floor held fixed, the two series are consistent with the task-based prediction on the margin where wages could not adjust.

\section{Framework and Derivations}\label{app:theory}

This appendix supports Sections~\ref{sec:strategy} and~\ref{sec:results}. In Section~\ref{sec:results} we report that employment in more automatable occupations fell after 2016 while their mean pay rose, and that exposure to the wage floor accounts for the pay movement. Those two facts look contradictory if we read pay as the price of labour in the occupation. Here we show that they are not. We write down the smallest model in which both occur together, and then we establish the four estimator properties the main text relies on. We carry out every step in full so that a reader can check the algebra without reconstructing it.

\subsection{The Environment}

We consider occupation $i$ observed in release $t$. The occupation contains a continuum of employee jobs indexed by $j$. Pay in job $j$ is the product of an occupation-level wage index and a job-specific component,
\begin{equation}
w_{ijt} = \omega_{it}\,\epsilon_j ,
\end{equation}
where $\omega_{it} > 0$ is the wage index and $\epsilon_j > 0$ is drawn from a distribution with cumulative distribution function $G_i$ and density $g_i$, fixed over time within the occupation. The multiplicative form is what lets a single index summarise the occupation's price of labour while leaving the shape of its internal pay distribution free.

Labour demand for the occupation is
\begin{equation}
N^{d}_{it} = D_i\!\left(\omega_{it};\, z_{it}\right), \qquad z_{it} \equiv \tau_t A_i ,
\end{equation}
where $\tau_t \geq 0$ indexes the state of automation technology available in release $t$ and $A_i \in [0,1]$ is the share of the occupation's tasks that the technology can perform. The ONS probability measures $A_i$. Demand is decreasing in the wage index and in the automation term,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial D_i}{\partial \omega} < 0, \qquad \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial z} < 0 .
\end{equation}
Labour supply to the occupation is
\begin{equation}
N^{s}_{it} = S_i\!\left(\omega_{it}\right), \qquad S_i' > 0 .
\end{equation}
A statutory floor $\bar{w}_t$ applies to every job, so pay as observed in the published statistics is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:obs}
w^{\mathrm{obs}}_{ijt} = \max\!\left(\omega_{it}\,\epsilon_j,\; \bar{w}_t\right).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:obs} is the only place the floor enters, and it is what separates the price of labour from the statistic we observe.

\subsection{Comparative Statics of an Automation Shock}

Suppose first that the floor does not bind anywhere in the occupation, so that observed pay equals $\omega_{it}\epsilon_j$ throughout. Equilibrium requires
\begin{equation}
D_i\!\left(\omega_{it};\, z_{it}\right) = S_i\!\left(\omega_{it}\right).
\end{equation}
Totally differentiating with respect to $z$ and collecting terms in $d\omega$ gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial D_i}{\partial \omega}\, d\omega + \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial z}\, dz = S_i'\, d\omega
\qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad
\frac{d\omega}{dz} = \frac{\partial D_i / \partial z}{S_i' - \partial D_i / \partial \omega} .
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive because $S_i' > 0$ and $\partial D_i / \partial \omega < 0$, and the numerator is negative, so $d\omega / dz < 0$. Employment follows from the supply curve,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN_i}{dz} = S_i'\, \frac{d\omega}{dz} < 0 .
\end{equation}
Writing $\varepsilon^{S}_i$ for the elasticity of supply, $\varepsilon^{D}_i$ for the absolute elasticity of demand, and $\phi_i \equiv \partial \ln D_i / \partial z < 0$ for the proportional demand shift, the same two results in elasticity form are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elast}
\frac{d \ln \omega_i}{dz} = \frac{\phi_i}{\varepsilon^{S}_i + \varepsilon^{D}_i},
\qquad
\frac{d \ln N_i}{dz} = \frac{\varepsilon^{S}_i\, \phi_i}{\varepsilon^{S}_i + \varepsilon^{D}_i} .
\end{equation}
Both are negative, and their ratio is the supply elasticity, $d \ln N_i / d \ln \omega_i = \varepsilon^{S}_i$. An automation shock with no floor therefore lowers pay and employment together. Our pay margin fails to show that prediction.

Now suppose instead that the floor binds at the margin, so that the wage index cannot fall. Setting $d \ln \omega_i = 0$ in the demand curve leaves the whole adjustment on the quantity margin,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bind}
\left.\frac{d \ln N_i}{dz}\right|_{\text{floor binds}} = \phi_i .
\end{equation}
Comparing \eqref{eq:bind} with \eqref{eq:elast} gives a sharp prediction, since $\varepsilon^{S}_i / (\varepsilon^{S}_i + \varepsilon^{D}_i) < 1$ implies
\begin{equation}
\left| \left.\frac{d \ln N_i}{dz}\right|_{\text{floor binds}} \right| \; > \; \left| \left.\frac{d \ln N_i}{dz}\right|_{\text{floor slack}} \right| .
\end{equation}
The employment loss from a given automation shock should be larger where the floor binds. We test this in Table~\ref{tab:jobs} column 5 by interacting floor exposure with the post-2016 indicator in the employment regression. The coefficient is $0.099$ with a standard error of $0.059$ and an interval of $[-0.016, 0.215]$. We cannot distinguish it from zero, and its point estimate carries the opposite sign to the prediction. We record the prediction as unsupported. Two features of the setting would attenuate or reverse it, namely that the floor also raises the cost of labour in exposed occupations relative to their own counterfactual, and that we measure the exposure share at a single date, but we test neither here.

\subsection{The Floor-Exposure Share as the First-Order Effect on Mean Pay}

In equation~\eqref{eq:nlw} we use the share of an occupation's jobs paid below the incoming floor as the measure of exposure. We now derive that choice. Define the occupation's mean observed pay from \eqref{eq:obs}, holding the wage index and the distribution $G_i$ fixed,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mdef}
m_{it} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\max\!\left(\omega_{it}\epsilon,\; \bar{w}_t\right)\right]
\;=\; \int_{0}^{a_{it}} \bar{w}_t \, dG_i(\epsilon) \;+\; \int_{a_{it}}^{\infty} \omega_{it}\,\epsilon \, dG_i(\epsilon),
\qquad a_{it} \equiv \frac{\bar{w}_t}{\omega_{it}},
\end{equation}
and define the share of jobs paid at or below the floor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fdef}
F_{it} \;=\; \Pr\!\left(\omega_{it}\epsilon \leq \bar{w}_t\right) \;=\; G_i\!\left(a_{it}\right).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:floor}
Holding the wage index $\omega_{it}$ and the distribution $G_i$ fixed, the derivative of mean observed pay with respect to the floor equals the share of jobs at or below the floor,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial m_{it}}{\partial \bar{w}_t} \;=\; F_{it}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate the two terms of \eqref{eq:mdef} in turn, noting that $\partial a_{it} / \partial \bar{w}_t = 1/\omega_{it}$. The first term is $\bar{w}_t\, G_i(a_{it})$, and by the product rule
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \bar{w}_t}\left[\bar{w}_t\, G_i(a_{it})\right]
= G_i(a_{it}) + \bar{w}_t\, g_i(a_{it})\,\frac{1}{\omega_{it}} .
\end{equation}
The second term is $\omega_{it}\int_{a_{it}}^{\infty}\epsilon\, dG_i(\epsilon)$, and by the Leibniz rule, since the upper limit is fixed and the lower limit moves,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \bar{w}_t}\left[\omega_{it}\int_{a_{it}}^{\infty}\epsilon\, dG_i(\epsilon)\right]
= -\,\omega_{it}\, a_{it}\, g_i(a_{it})\,\frac{1}{\omega_{it}}
= -\,\bar{w}_t\, g_i(a_{it})\,\frac{1}{\omega_{it}},
\end{equation}
where the last equality substitutes $\omega_{it} a_{it} = \bar{w}_t$ from \eqref{eq:mdef}. The two density terms are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign, so they cancel, and adding the two derivatives leaves
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial m_{it}}{\partial \bar{w}_t} = G_i(a_{it}) = F_{it},
\end{equation}
which is the claim. The cancellation is the statement that a job exactly at the floor is indifferent between the two branches of \eqref{eq:obs}, so moving it across the boundary changes nothing.
\end{proof}

Two consequences matter for our empirical work. First, differentiating once more,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^{2} m_{it}}{\partial \bar{w}_t^{2}} = \frac{g_i(a_{it})}{\omega_{it}} \;\geq\; 0,
\end{equation}
so mean observed pay is convex in the floor. A discrete uprating from $\bar{w}_0$ to $\bar{w}_1$ therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete}
m(\bar{w}_1) - m(\bar{w}_0) \;=\; \int_{\bar{w}_0}^{\bar{w}_1} F(t)\, dt \;\geq\; F(\bar{w}_0)\left(\bar{w}_1 - \bar{w}_0\right),
\end{equation}
because $F$ is increasing. Measuring the share before the uprating, as we do in equation~\eqref{eq:nlw}, therefore understates the effect of a discrete rise. Second, our regression uses log mean pay. The object the coefficient $\theta$ approximates is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \ln m_{it}}{\partial F_{it}} \;\approx\; \frac{\bar{w}_1 - \bar{w}_0}{m_{it}},
\end{equation}
which varies across occupations with the level of mean pay. Evaluating it at $F_i = 1$, where the whole of the occupation's pay distribution lies below the incoming floor, gives a bound that needs no knowledge of $\omega_i$ or $G_i$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bound}
For an occupation with $F_i = 1$ in the base release, the change in log mean observed pay between the base release and release $t$ is at least $\ln\!\left(\bar{w}_t / \bar{w}_{2016}\right)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $F_i = 1$ then every job in the occupation is paid below $\bar{w}_{2016}$ in the base release, so $m_{i,2015} \leq \bar{w}_{2016}$. In release $t$ the floor applies to every job, so $m_{it} \geq \bar{w}_t$ by \eqref{eq:obs}. Taking logs of the ratio gives
\begin{equation}
\ln m_{it} - \ln m_{i,2015} \;\geq\; \ln \bar{w}_t - \ln \bar{w}_{2016},
\end{equation}
which is the claim.
\end{proof}

The NLW rates in April of 2016 to 2020 were \pounds 7.20, \pounds 7.50, \pounds 7.83, \pounds 8.21 and \pounds 8.72. The implied per-release lower bounds are $0.000$, $0.041$, $0.084$, $0.131$ and $0.192$ log points, and their average over the five post-2016 releases is $0.090$. Since $\theta$ in equation~\eqref{eq:nlw} is the coefficient on a demeaned share and the post-2016 indicator pools those five releases, the estimate of $\theta$ extrapolated to $F_i = 1$ should be at least $0.090$, provided that occupations with no jobs below the floor experience no floor-driven growth of their own. The estimate in Table~\ref{tab:nlw} column 2 is $0.164$ with a standard error of $0.017$. It lies above the bound. Because the third branch of equation~\eqref{eq:share} caps the share at the highest published rank, no occupation in the sample attains $F_i = 1$, and the comparison extrapolates the linear coefficient to that point. The excess is consistent with two mechanisms. The first is spillovers above the floor within exposed occupations, and \citet{giupponi2024minimum} document such spillovers up to roughly the twentieth percentile over these years. The second is the composition term of Proposition~\ref{prop:composition}. Spillovers that reached occupations with no jobs below the floor would work in the other direction by raising the growth of the comparison group. The bound serves as a benchmark only.

\subsection{Composition and the Occupational Mean}

The published statistic is the mean pay of whoever holds the occupation's jobs in that release. A change in it mixes pay growth for continuing jobs with a change in which jobs exist. We now separate the two exactly. Let the occupation hold $N_{t-1}$ jobs in release $t-1$ with mean pay $m_{t-1}$. Let $E$ of those jobs disappear by release $t$, with mean pay $m^{L}_{t-1}$ measured at $t-1$, and let each of the $N_t = N_{t-1} - E$ surviving jobs change pay by $\Delta w_j$. Suppose for the moment that no new jobs enter.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:composition}
The change in the occupation's mean pay decomposes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
m_t - m_{t-1} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{j \in \mathrm{stayers}} \Delta w_j}_{\text{pay growth of continuing jobs}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{E}{N_t}\left(m_{t-1} - m^{L}_{t-1}\right)}_{\text{composition}} .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The total wage bill in release $t$ is the bill at $t-1$, less the bill of the jobs that left, plus the pay changes of those that stayed,
\begin{equation}
N_t\, m_t \;=\; N_{t-1}\, m_{t-1} \;-\; E\, m^{L}_{t-1} \;+\; \sum_{j \in \mathrm{stayers}} \Delta w_j .
\end{equation}
Divide by $N_t$ and substitute $N_{t-1} = N_t + E$,
\begin{equation}
m_t \;=\; m_{t-1} + \frac{E}{N_t}\, m_{t-1} - \frac{E}{N_t}\, m^{L}_{t-1} + \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{j \in \mathrm{stayers}} \Delta w_j ,
\end{equation}
and collecting the two terms in $E / N_t$ gives \eqref{eq:decomp}.
\end{proof}

The composition term is positive whenever the jobs that disappear paid less than the occupation's average. That is the case when a contraction concentrates at the bottom of an occupation's pay distribution. A fall in employment can therefore raise measured mean pay with no worker receiving a rise. Allowing entry adds a symmetric term. If $B$ jobs enter at mean pay $m^{B}_{t}$, the decomposition gains $(B / N_t)(m^{B}_{t} - m_{t-1})$. That term is negative when entrants earn less than the incumbent average.

Section~\ref{sec:results} reports that across 324 occupations the change in log employment from 2015 to 2019 correlates $-0.37$ with the automation probability while the change in log mean pay correlates $+0.36$. That pairing is what \eqref{eq:decomp} produces when exit concentrates at the bottom. The published series give $m_t$, $m_{t-1}$, $N_t$ and $N_{t-1}$ but not $m^{L}_{t-1}$ or the individual $\Delta w_j$. We cannot separate the two terms here. The ASHE microdata identify both and would close this gap.

\subsection{Properties of the Estimators}

The remaining propositions justify four statements we make in Section~\ref{sec:strategy} about what the regressions recover.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:deflator}
Adding any release-specific constant to the dependent variable leaves every coefficient on an interaction with a release indicator, or with a function of $t$, unchanged in equations~\eqref{eq:twfe}, \eqref{eq:es}, \eqref{eq:trend} and \eqref{eq:nlw}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $P_t$ denote a price index and write real log pay as $\ln w_{it} - \ln P_t$. Substituting into equation~\eqref{eq:twfe} gives
\begin{equation}
\ln w_{it} - \ln P_t = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta \left( A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2016] \right) + \varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}
which rearranges to
\begin{equation}
\ln w_{it} = \alpha_i + \left(\lambda_t + \ln P_t\right) + \beta \left( A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2016] \right) + \varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
The release effects are unrestricted, so $\lambda_t + \ln P_t$ is as admissible a set of release effects as $\lambda_t$, and the least-squares problem in $\beta$ is unchanged. The same substitution works for the other three equations because in each the deflator enters only through a term that depends on $t$ alone. Deflating therefore shifts the release effects and nothing else.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:static}
In equation~\eqref{eq:twfe} with a balanced panel of $T_0$ pre-treatment and $T_1$ post-treatment releases and equal weights, the static coefficient satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fwl}
\hat{\beta} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(\bar{y}^{\,\mathrm{post}}_i - \bar{y}^{\,\mathrm{pre}}_i,\; A_i\right)}{\mathrm{Var}\!\left(A_i\right)}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:esavg}
\hat{\beta} = \frac{1}{T_1}\sum_{s \geq 2016} \hat{\beta}_s \;-\; \frac{1}{T_0}\sum_{s < 2016} \hat{\beta}_s ,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\beta}_s$ are the event-study coefficients of equation~\eqref{eq:es} with any base release.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For \eqref{eq:fwl}, apply the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem. Residualising the regressor $A_i P_t$ on occupation and release effects in a balanced panel gives $\left(A_i - \bar{A}\right)\left(P_t - \bar{P}\right)$, because $A_i$ is constant within an occupation and $P_t$ within a release. The least-squares coefficient of $y_{it}$ on that residual is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_i \sum_t \left(A_i - \bar{A}\right)\left(P_t - \bar{P}\right) y_{it}}{\sum_i \sum_t \left(A_i - \bar{A}\right)^{2}\left(P_t - \bar{P}\right)^{2}} .
\end{equation}
Summing over $t$ first, and using $\sum_t (P_t - \bar{P}) y_{it} = T_0 T_1 (\bar{y}^{\,\mathrm{post}}_i - \bar{y}^{\,\mathrm{pre}}_i) / (T_0 + T_1)$, the common factor cancels between numerator and denominator and leaves \eqref{eq:fwl}. For \eqref{eq:esavg}, note that equation~\eqref{eq:es} saturates the interaction of $A_i$ with the release indicators, so it fits the release-specific gradients exactly. The static regressor $A_i P_t$ is the projection of that saturated set onto $P_t$, and a projection onto a binary regressor with equal weights within each period returns the difference of the two period averages of the fitted gradients, which is \eqref{eq:esavg}.
\end{proof}

Our estimates satisfy \eqref{eq:esavg} numerically. For pay, the post-period average of the coefficients in Table~\ref{tab:espay} column 1 is $(0.051 + 0.073 + 0.101 + 0.151 + 0.125)/5 = 0.100$, the pre-period average with 2015 set to zero is $(-0.043 + 0)/2 = -0.021$, and the difference is $0.122$. That figure is the static estimate in Table~\ref{tab:pay} column 1. For employment on the balanced panel, the same calculation gives $-0.435 - 0.048 = -0.483$. That figure matches the estimate in Table~\ref{tab:jobs} column 2 to three decimals. The pay identity holds to rounding because the pay panel is nearly balanced, with 2,546 of the $366 \times 7 = 2{,}562$ possible cells present, whereas the employment panel used here is balanced by construction.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:restriction}
Equation~\eqref{eq:trend} is the restriction
\begin{equation}
\beta_s = \gamma\,(s - 2015) + \delta\, \mathbf{1}[s \geq 2016]
\end{equation}
imposed on the event-study coefficients of equation~\eqref{eq:es}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute the restriction into the summation in \eqref{eq:es} and collect terms,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{s \neq 2015} \beta_s \left(A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t=s]\right)
= \gamma \sum_{s \neq 2015} (s-2015) \left(A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t=s]\right) + \delta \sum_{s \geq 2016} \left(A_i \times \mathbf{1}[t=s]\right),
\end{equation}
and note that $\sum_{s}(s-2015)\mathbf{1}[t=s] = t - 2015$ and $\sum_{s \geq 2016}\mathbf{1}[t=s] = \mathbf{1}[t \geq 2016]$, which reproduces \eqref{eq:trend}.
\end{proof}

With one pre-treatment release the two parameters are identified from different features of the data. The trend $\gamma$ is identified from the slope across all seven releases, whereas the break $\delta$ is identified from the departure of the post-2016 coefficients from the line through the two pre-treatment points, so it rests on a single pre-period contrast. That is why the standard error on $\delta$ in Table~\ref{tab:trend} is about three times the standard error on $\gamma$, and why with this pre-period we can rule a break out only weakly while still establishing the trend.

\subsection{What the Static Coefficient Identifies}

\citet{callaway2024continuous} study exactly the design of equation~\eqref{eq:twfe} when the treatment is a continuous dose. Write $Y_{it}(a)$ for the outcome occupation $i$ would record in release $t$ under dose $a$, and define the average causal response at dose $a$ as the derivative of $\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}(a) - Y_{it}(0)]$ with respect to $a$. They show that the cross-sectional slope in \eqref{eq:fwl} equals a weighted average of that derivative only when the counterfactual change in the untreated outcome does not depend on the dose, which they call strong parallel trends,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spt}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left. Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,t-1}(0) \;\right|\; A_i = a \right] \; \text{does not vary with } a .
\end{equation}
When \eqref{eq:spt} fails, the slope in \eqref{eq:fwl} adds a selection term equal to the slope of the counterfactual change on the dose,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\beta} \;\xrightarrow{p}\; \underbrace{\text{weighted average causal response}}_{\text{the object of interest}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left. Y_{it}(0) - Y_{i,t-1}(0) \;\right|\; A_i = a \right]}_{\text{selection on the counterfactual path}} .
\end{equation}
Condition \eqref{eq:spt} fails for pay in this setting by construction, since the dose correlates $-0.80$ with the 2014 level of pay and the level of pay determines exposure to a floor that rose throughout the post period. Equation~\eqref{eq:nlw} removes the part of the selection term that runs through floor exposure. For that reason we read the risk coefficient there. It does not remove selection running through any other correlate of the dose, and accordingly we report every static interaction as descriptive.

\subsection{The Floor-Exposure Share}

The ONS does not publish the share of an occupation's jobs paid below the incoming floor. We interpolate it from the percentiles the ONS does publish. For occupation $i$ in the 2015 release, let $(q_k, p_k)$ for $k = 1, \dots, K$ denote the published percentile values and their ranks, including the median at rank 50, sorted in increasing order of value, with any suppressed percentile omitted. Treating the pay distribution as piecewise linear between adjacent published percentiles, the share of jobs paid below a threshold $\bar{w}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:share}
F_i(\bar{w}) \;=\; \frac{1}{100}
\begin{cases}
\max\!\left\{0,\; p_1 - \dfrac{p_2 - p_1}{q_2 - q_1}\left(q_1 - \bar{w}\right)\right\}, & \bar{w} \leq q_1, \\[14pt]
p_k + \dfrac{p_{k+1} - p_k}{q_{k+1} - q_k}\left(\bar{w} - q_k\right), & q_k < \bar{w} \leq q_{k+1}, \\[14pt]
p_K, & \bar{w} > q_K .
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
We set $\bar{w} = 7.20$, the NLW rate of April 2016. The middle branch is ordinary linear interpolation within the bracketing segment. The first branch extrapolates below the lowest published percentile using the slope of the lowest published segment and clamps the result at zero. An occupation whose tenth percentile lies far above the floor thus receives a share of zero where the linear extrapolation would return a negative number. The third branch assigns the highest published rank when the threshold exceeds the ninetieth percentile. We compute the share only where the ONS publishes at least three percentiles. That condition holds for 347 of the 366 occupations. The Kaitz index is $\bar{w} / q_{50}$ and needs no interpolation.

A reader can check two worked examples by hand against Table 14.5a of the 2015 release. Sales and retail assistants, code 7111, have a median of \pounds 7.11 at rank 50 and a sixtieth percentile of \pounds 7.41, so the threshold falls in the segment from rank 50 to rank 60 and the middle branch of \eqref{eq:share} gives
\begin{equation}
F_{7111} = \frac{1}{100}\left[50 + \frac{60 - 50}{7.41 - 7.11}\left(7.20 - 7.11\right)\right] = \frac{1}{100}\left[50 + 3.0\right] = 0.530 .
\end{equation}
Medical practitioners, code 2211, have a tenth percentile of \pounds 15.54 and a twentieth of \pounds 20.19, so the threshold lies below the lowest published percentile and the first branch applies,
\begin{equation}
F_{2211} = \frac{1}{100}\max\!\left\{0,\; 10 - \frac{20 - 10}{20.19 - 15.54}\left(15.54 - 7.20\right)\right\} = \frac{1}{100}\max\!\left\{0,\; -7.94\right\} = 0 .
\end{equation}
The two occupations sit at the extremes of the exposure distribution. The correlation of $0.60$ between the share and the automation probability reflects that spread.
